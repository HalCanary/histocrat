All dates use the Holocene calendar, whose years are marked HE (Holocene Era), and which adds 10,000 to the CE year.  (The Holocene calendar was proposed by the geologist Cesare Emiliani in 1993, so that all of human history since the end of the last ice age could be counted in positive years.)  For example, 2026 CE is 12026 HE, and 5001 BCE is 5000 HE.  The federation (see The Nation) uses hexadecimal numbers, but this document gives numbers in decimal unless written with a 0x prefix (so 0x100 means hex 100, or 256).

A small number of well-trained time travelers are sent back in time to 5000 HE (5001 BCE).  They are only allowed to take with them what they can carry.  They choose the island of Cyprus for their target.  Their goals are

1.  Alter the general course of history by introducing a more moral and just society before introducing the technological changes that have led to some of the worst parts of history.

2.  Document every culture and language they come in contact with.  Map out what humanity was like in the sixth millennium HE, both the farming peoples who will grow into the first civilizations, documented from their very beginnings, and those still living in small hunter-gatherer bands.

3.  Document everything their society accomplishes, leaving an incredible record for future historians.

In addition to basic supplies, they carry seeds and books.  They intend to live peacefully among the local population and invite them to join their society.

## The Travelers

The travelers come from a future post-scarcity world.  There are a few hundred of them.  The trip is one-way: their arrival creates a new branch of time, so there is no future to return to, and they don't have the technology to build a time machine anyway.

Their society has advanced technology and biological knowledge, and their bodies are already "perfected" by 21st-century standards, so the diseases of the ancient world are not a serious threat to them.  They train and practice together for years before leaving.  Before they arrive, the founders agree on a constitution, which allows for its own amendment.

The founders think of themselves as immigrants to this land, not as its rulers.  Their goal is an egalitarian society, and they do not set themselves up as a ruling class above the people who join them.

Within a few generations, the travelers and the local people have intermarried so thoroughly that there is no separate founder population.  The new population has, on average, fewer genetic defects than its neighbors.

### The Books

The travelers bring a small library of books.  Each volume runs 150 to 250 pages; most of the books fill a single volume, and the whole library is about 38 volumes, some 7,000 pages.  All of them are written in the basic archival language (see Records), with technical terms defined as needed, so that anyone who learns the archival language can read them.  They include:

* **Hygiene and basic medicine** (two volumes), designed for this technology level.  Its first page gives the recipe for oral rehydration solution for treating diarrhea, probably the single most life-saving thing in the book: to a jug of clean, boiled water, add a small measured amount of salt and about ten times as much honey or grape syrup (or use the water from boiled grain), with exact measures given in the book.

* **Germ theory**, including a set of experiments anyone can perform to gather evidence for it, such as comparing how quickly boiled and unboiled broth spoils in sealed and open pots, or mapping cases of illness against the wells and streams people drink from.

* **Basic engineering** (three volumes): general principles, with plans for useful machines such as waterwheels, ox carts, wheelbarrows, spinning wheels, looms, and mills; building with neolithic materials (stone, brick, timber, and lime), including arches, cisterns, granaries, drains, and building to survive earthquakes; and insulating kilns and furnaces so that they use much less fuel.

* **Chemistry**: elements and compounds, the conservation of mass, and the experiments that lead to atomic theory, with a table of the few dozen most common elements; and the chemistry behind the crafts, such as lime, soap, dyes and mordants, fermentation, distillation, and smelting.

* **Metallurgy**: copper, bronze, and brass.

* **Glass and optics**: how to make glass, beads, vessels, glazes, and mirrors; how to grind lenses, from a simple bead microscope to reading glasses and telescopes; and instruments such as thermometers and hourglasses.

* **Sailboats**: hull design, rigging, and seamanship, from a small catboat that can sail against the wind up to sloops and schooners, all built with neolithic tools and materials: planks joined without nails, seams sealed with pine resin, and sails and rope of flax.

* **Atlas**: physical maps of the world (as it is around 5000 HE) and of its regions, showing only elevation, seas, lakes, rivers, and lines of latitude and longitude.

* **Military affairs**: strategy, tactics, logistics, and training.  The real revolution here is not any weapon, but the idea of methodical, regular military training.

* **The archival language**: its alphabet and letter forms, the numbering of its characters, and its rules of grammar and syntax (see Records).

* **Dictionary**: a complete basic dictionary of the archival language.

* **Writing and records**: how to make clay tablets, paper, ink, and pens; how to print and bind books; and the conventions every copy follows, from the title page to proofreading (see Records).

* **Phonetics and phonology**: how each sound is produced (by the lips, tongue, palate, nose, and larynx), illustrated with cross-section drawings of the head, including voicing, tone, stress, and length; and the method for working out which sound differences matter in a given language (its phonemes), by finding pairs of words that differ in only one sound.  Vowels in particular can't be learned fully from a book, so the founders teach the reference sounds by ear.

* **Basic mathematics**: arithmetic, geometry, basic algebra (variables, functions, and Cartesian coordinates), and logic (see Appendix: Mathematics).  Arithmetic uses positional numerals with a zero, in hexadecimal (base 16), and people are taught to count in binary on their fingers.

* **Intermediate mathematics**: trigonometry, logarithms, probability and statistics, and set theory, with tables of trigonometric functions and logarithms.

* **Astronomy, the calendar, and timekeeping** (two volumes): enough to predict the motions of the sun, moon, planets, and stars, which keeps the calendar running even when clouds make it impossible to observe the sky.  This includes the reasons why observations support a heliocentric model, and where a geocentric model fails, for example in explaining the motion of Mars.  It also includes the equation of time.

* **Weights and measures**: a system built in factors of 4 and 16, with units that any town can rebuild from scratch with bronze-age tools: a string, a stone, and a jug of water (see Appendix: Units).  (Unit names here are placeholders.)
  * **Angle**: the turn, one full circle, divided by 16 again and again: the hour (1/16 turn), the degree (1/256 turn, about 1.4 of our degrees), and the second (1/65536 turn).
  * **Time**: the day, divided the same way, since the sky turns through one hour of angle in one hour of time: the hour (1/16 day, 90 minutes), the degree (1/256 day, about 5.6 minutes), and the second (1/65536 day, about 1.3 seconds).  See Records for how dates and times are written.
  * **Length**: the reach, about 1.729 m, roughly an arm span.  It is the length of a pendulum whose single swing takes one second, so it can be found by counting 4,096 swings over an hour.  The founders bring a brass standard artifact.
  * **Volume**: the jug, a cube one hand (1/16 reach) on a side, about 1.26 liters.  A quarter jug is a cup, and 1/256 jug is a spoon, about a teaspoon.
  * **Mass**: a jug of water, about 1.26 kg.  The founders bring a brass standard artifact.

* **Agriculture and ecology** (two volumes): how plants draw nutrients from the soil, and how rotating crops (including legumes) and returning manure and compost restore them; forestry, including coppicing (cutting trees back to the stump on a regular cycle, so they regrow as fuel without being replanted) and how to estimate how much wood a forest can sustainably yield; and selective breeding of plants and animals: how to choose and record which individuals to breed from, with suggested long-term projects, such as breeding woolly sheep from the hairy sheep of the period.  The book points to the founders' own seeds as an example of what long breeding programs can achieve.

* **Macroeconomics**: a simplified explanation (see Records).

* **Business**: how to run a workshop, a trading venture, or a partnership, including contracts, loans, pricing, and double-entry bookkeeping, taught through worked examples such as a potter's workshop and a merchant's voyage.

* **Ethics** (three volumes): aimed at different ages and readers (see Religion and Ethics).

* **Fiction** (five volumes): stories retold in the archival language, such as fables, folk tales, adventures, and love stories, chosen to be timeless in their setting, so that they make sense to a farmer in a neolithic village and will still make sense to readers thousands of years later.  They also give students of the archival language something enjoyable to read.

* **Poetry and songs**: a small collection composed in the archival language.  Poetry is hard to write in a simplified language with no idioms, and the collection doesn't pretend otherwise; its main purpose is to show what poems and songs are possible, and to encourage people to make more in their own everyday languages.

* **Music**: a notation for melody, harmony, and rhythm, precise enough to record the music of any people (see Appendix: Music Notation), followed by several tunes written out in it, including the melodies for the songs in the poetry collection.  The book also covers scales and harmony.  It gives plans for a few simple instruments that can be made with the materials available: a guitar with gut strings and frets placed for equal temperament, a flute of cane, wood, or bone with its finger holes placed the same way, a trumpet of brass or wood (like the militia's signal horns, described under Military, it has no valves, so it plays only its natural notes), and drums.  The founders don't try to supply every instrument; people will invent the others soon enough.

* **Games**: a collection of games with simple rules and deep strategy, playable with pebbles, seeds, dice, or clay tiles: Go, Hex, mancala, nine men's morris, Nim, backgammon, and dominoes, along with group games such as liar's dice, and five-card draw and Texas hold'em for a deck of 64 tiles.  Each game includes notes on strategy, and the dice and tile games include tables of the odds.  (With 16 tiles in each suit, a straight is rarer than a flush, so it ranks higher.)

* **Government and law**: the constitution, plus a list of basic default laws.

* **Town planning and expansion**: how to lay out a town, when and how to found a new one, and how the federation should grow.  It is worded as a set of suggestions, each with its justification, so that councils (see Tables) understand why the plan makes sense and can adapt it sensibly when there are exceptions, such as unusual geography.

One of the first projects is copying the books onto clay tablets.  Each town has a public library holding a full set of the founding books on clay.  Within a few generations, the libraries hold many more books than just the founding texts, as the society begins writing its own.  Anyone who owns a kiln can fire a book in clay, and every town runs a large kiln for brickmaking, so in practice the decision of what to make permanent is widely shared.

Since books are copied onto clay anyway, they are also often fired as large tiles and used to cover the outside walls of buildings.  A building's walls may carry the text of a whole book, readable by anyone passing by.

### The Seeds

The travelers bring seeds for whatever crops make sense for Cyprus's Mediterranean climate, in improved modern varieties, the results of thousands of years of selective breeding: wheat, barley, lentils, chickpeas, olives, grapes, figs, flax, canola, and so on.

They also bring crops from the Western Hemisphere, which won't otherwise reach the Old World for more than six thousand years.  They are chosen to add variety to a diet built on grain and pulses, and all of them grow well in Cyprus today: tomatoes, peppers (both sweet and chili), squash and pumpkins, common beans, potatoes, and sunflowers.

In addition, they bring a few genetically engineered plants: a bush whose leaves contain acetaminophen, for pain and fever, and a "nutrition bush" whose leaves, brewed as a tea, supply vitamins and iron for pregnant women and children.  Medicinal tea plants must be engineered for a consistent strength, since a tea's dose varies with the leaf and the steeping, and come with clear dosing instructions.

Many useful medicines need no engineering at all.  The medicine book describes natural remedies the travelers can grow or find on Cyprus: willow bark for pain and fever, chamomile and mint for digestion, thyme and sage for coughs and sore throats, valerian for sleep, and senna as a laxative.  They bring seeds of sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*), whose tea helps against malaria, which is common on the island.  The book warns against making teas from plants with a narrow margin between a helpful and a fatal dose, such as foxglove or opium poppy.

Besides seeds, they bring sealed vials of dried cultures: a high-yield strain of *Penicillium* mold, along with yeast for bread and brewing, cultures for cheese and yogurt, and a vinegar culture.  A reserve of the original dried spores is kept in the vault, and working cultures are restarted from it regularly, since cultures lose their strength over many generations.  At first, the *Penicillium* can only be used as a crude, freshly made filtrate on wounds, burns, and skin and eye infections.  Purified penicillin that can cure infections inside the body requires chemistry and sterile injection that are centuries away; the medicine book describes how to make it when the time comes.

## Why Cyprus

Around 5000 HE, Cyprus is thinly populated.  The local people live in small groups that can easily be absorbed into a founding population of a few hundred, rather than the travelers being absorbed into them.  The island is also separated by sea from the larger populations of the mainland, and has copper in its mountains.  In the early centuries, much of its protection comes simply from obscurity: few people on the mainland know the federation exists, and fewer could reach it.  That changes once its ports open and its ships and goods spread its reputation.

## Early Technology

Some technologies are introduced right away because they are easy and their benefits are overwhelmingly positive: hygiene and basic medicine (clean water, washing, wound care, keeping waste away from water sources), paper, and copper working.

Other technologies are held back.  Iron and gunpowder are not introduced; they wait until someone else discovers them.  But once the federation learns that iron has been discovered elsewhere, it works to get an iron industry going quickly, and once it is started, spreads the technology to every town.

Bronze is not available until trade routes reach the tin mines, since Cyprus has no tin.  The travelers know where the tin deposits are, but their latitudes and longitudes are kept secret, so that the knowledge doesn't spread to the neighbors.  The federation knows where to look once it can gather the resources for an expedition.  Even so, it could take generations.  Until then, copper is common enough for pots, ornaments, and mirrors, but too soft to replace stone for most tools.  Brass helps fill the gap: Cyprus's copper ores naturally contain zinc, so the federation can make a harder copper alloy from its own ores, long before the tin routes open.  Even so, most everyday tools stay stone, bone, and wood, as in the real early bronze age, when bronze went to weapons, ornaments, and elite goods.

Apart from these few technologies, no knowledge is kept secret.  (The only other exception is the glaze recipes used for tax tokens, kept secret by the mint to prevent counterfeiting; see Records.)  But there is only so much the travelers can teach, so they prioritize science that can be tested locally and technology that is immediately useful.  Things like nuclear physics are a low priority.

Cyprus has no cattle at the time of the arrival; they were brought to the island in the early Neolithic but later died out.  Since the town needs oxen for plowing, hauling, and turning mills, a special trip is made to the mainland in the first decades to bring back breeding cattle.  Calves and young cows are easier to carry by boat than full-grown animals.

The travelers also introduce the plow, about a thousand years before it would otherwise appear.  The first plows are simple ards (scratch plows) of hardwood, possibly with a stone or copper tip, pulled by a pair of oxen.  They don't turn the soil over, but they let one farmer break far more ground than hoeing by hand, which is what lets a town feed a few thousand people from fields within walking distance.

The first things taught come straight from the books: germ theory and hygiene, soil and crop rotation, hexadecimal arithmetic, geometry for surveying, and enough astronomy to keep the calendar.

## The Town

Until the industrial revolution, most people will be farmers, and therefore, the core of this new civilization will be the agricultural town.  For safety, each town will build town walls, which requires a certain minimum population to maintain and defend those walls.  Permanent residential communities outside the walls are prohibited, for three reasons.  The first is defense: anyone living in an outlying village is not protected by the walls.  The second is cultural cohesion.  Everyone in a town shares the same school, library, assembly, militia, and market, and meets the rest of the town every week.  A scattering of small villages would each drift apart in language, customs, and literacy, and would be too small to support a school or a library of its own.  The third is representative local government.  The head of a table (see Tables) in town can spend an hour at the assembly every week; the head of an outlying village, a long walk away, could not.  Since everyone lives inside the walls, all of a town's farmland must lie within a daily walk of the town, and this puts a maximum size on the town.  Depending on the quality of the farmland, this might support a town of a few thousand people.  The town's size is planned from its grain harvest alone; extra yield, from potatoes and other improved crops, doesn't make the town larger, but goes to support the cities and into the granary as a reserve for emergencies.  Outlying posts, such as watchtowers, relay stations, mines, and shepherds' huts, are staffed in rotation: their crews may live there for weeks at a time, but their homes and tables are in a town, and no families settle there.  Small groups might also camp outside of town, such as a crew working on an outlying building.

Each town is planned out when it is founded, with a good idea of its eventual maximum population based on the farmland around it.  The streets, the line of the walls, and the sites of public buildings are laid out from the start, and the town fills in its plan as it grows.  The streets start as packed dirt, and are paved with cobbles or flagstones over time, as detail labor allows.  Paving is a lower priority than getting the fields planted and the houses built, so it can take a generation or more, starting with the main streets to the square and the gates.

Sanitation is a town-wide responsibility.  Rather than each house having its own cesspit, several households share a larger, sealed cesspit, built by the town.  The locations of wells, cisterns, cesspits, and buildings are all planned out together, as part of the town plan, by engineers trained in sanitation, so that every cesspit is well away from any source of drinking water.  These engineers are national officers: they rotate in to help a proconsul (see The Nation) plan a new town, then check on it periodically afterward.  The pits are emptied regularly by detail crews (see Tables), and their contents are composted outside the walls, downhill and away from water, for a year or two before being used as fertilizer on grain fields and orchards.  The engineering book includes designs for glazed ceramic pour-flush toilets, which will be built as soon as the town can support extra potters.

The town granary follows the engineering book's design, modeled on the Roman horrea: a raised floor and vents keep the grain dry and cool, and smooth plastered walls, rat-proof foundations, and the town's cats keep pests out.  Grain is stored in bins by year, so that the oldest is used first.

Grinding grain is also a town responsibility.  Instead of each household grinding its own flour by hand on a saddle quern, hours of hard work every day, each town has a mill, worked as detail labor like any other public project.  At first the millstones are turned by people, either pushing bars around a rotary mill or walking inside a treadwheel; once the town has oxen, they take over, and towns with a suitable stream build water mills.  Grain keeps for years in the granary, but flour goes stale within weeks, so the mill grinds from the granary as needed, and the weekly food allowance is mostly given out as flour, along with whole lentils, chickpeas, and some whole grain for porridge and brewing.  Tables don't pay for milling separately: the share of the harvest that the town keeps pays for it, so tables can see something concrete in return for the grain they hand over.  Baking stays with the tables, each of which has its own bread oven; some neighborhoods may also choose to share larger ovens to save firewood.

The land as a whole is managed as a single large forest, with each town's roughly circular farmland cut out of it like a cookie cutter.  A town together with all of the nearby land it governs (its fields, orchards, pastures, and town woodland) is called a **township**.  All forests are publicly owned: woodland within a township belongs to the town, and woodland outside every township belongs to the nation.  Townships usually don't touch, since a strip of nationally managed forest is kept between each pair of neighboring towns.  These strips may not be wide, but together they form a national forest.  Use of the national forest, for timber, fuel, hunting, and gathering, is allowed, but permission must be obtained first; the rangers (see Rangers) and the proconsuls work together on granting it, under forestry policy set by the Directorate (see The Nation).  Permission is always given for sustainable use.  For the purposes of the courts, the national land is roughly divided by nearest township, so a crime in the national forest is tried in the nearest town.  In practice, the arresting ranger is given latitude: "nearest" means nearest by the trails available to the ranger at the time.  The national forest supplies timber and fuel, separates the towns, and is the territory patrolled by the rangers.  Land is cleared along each road between towns, so that the roads also serve as firebreaks, and so that robbers have no cover to hide in beside the road.  A single row of trees along the road, with branches pruned high, gives travelers shade without giving robbers cover.  Like the town streets, the roads begin as level, well-drained packed dirt and are improved over time.  Extending the network comes first: every town gets its dirt road before any road is upgraded.  Then the busiest routes and the trouble spots are rebuilt as macadam, layers of graded gravel and crushed stone packed down by traffic, and only the most important roads are ever paved.

The walls grow over time.  In the first generations, while the island is nearly empty, they are low walls or even fences.  They mark the edge of the town and prevent unplanned sprawl, keep wild boar and deer out of gardens and stores, keep livestock in, and deter the occasional raid by local bands still hostile to the new town.  (Cyprus has no large predators.)  Once the federation begins trading by sea and becomes worth raiding, in the decades after the first port is founded (around 5400 HE), the walls are raised and strengthened into real defenses.  The ideal town has towers along its wall, each within bowshot of the next, so that archers in the towers can cover every stretch of wall between them.

A full-size town of 2,000–2,500 people needs roughly 15–18 hectares inside its walls (about 130–160 people per hectare, including houses, streets, the square, public buildings, workshops, gardens, and the yard).  That makes the walled town roughly 450 m across, with a wall about 1.5 km around.  By modern standards it is very walkable: nobody lives more than a four-minute walk from the square, and crossing the whole town takes about six minutes.  On an ideal site, the wall is a regular polygon with 32 (0x20) sides and a tower at each corner, so the towers are about 47 m apart, comfortably within bowshot; on real sites, the shape bends to fit the terrain.

Each town also has a large open yard inside the walls, about a hectare, which serves many purposes: it is the militia's drill ground, the threshing floor at harvest time, the ox yard where the working oxen are kept at night, and the livestock market on market days.  When the town is threatened, the herds are driven in through the gates and held in the yard, since livestock is the main thing raiders come for.  Packed in for a few days, the town's oxen and a couple of thousand sheep and goats fit easily; the town keeps enough fodder on hand to last out a short raid.  The yard is placed just inside the main gate, so that merchants can park their carts and pack animals there while they do business, without bringing them through the narrow residential streets; this also keeps their cargo next to the gate where the road tax (see Tables) is collected.  On one side of the yard is a town-run guesthouse, where traveling merchants, postmen (see Mail), and other visitors who don't belong to any of the town's tables can sleep, like the caravanserais of the Middle East.

There is a local precedent for walled settlements in Neolithic Cyprus: Khirokitia, the best-known Neolithic village on Cyprus (founded around 3000 HE), was surrounded by a massive stone wall, though it is uncertain whether it was built for defense or simply to mark the village's edge.

### Tables

The town is organized into "dinner tables": extended households small enough to eat together.  The table, not the individual family, is the basic unit of town life.  A full-size town has more than a hundred tables.

Each table has a leader, called the "head of the table," or simply "the head."  The head is chosen by the table's consensus.  In practice, when a dozen or so adults are given work to do together, a natural leader emerges to coordinate them, and that person becomes the head.  A head can be replaced the same informal way, whenever the table reaches a new consensus.  The town assembly consists of the heads of all the tables, and each head's vote in the assembly is proportional to the number of citizens in their table.  The assembly elects a much smaller town council, which does the day-to-day work of local government.  Since most heads already have to go to the town center every week to learn their tables' detail assignments, the assembly holds short, regular weekly meetings at the same time.  The mild Mediterranean weather means the assembly can usually meet outdoors in the town square, without needing a large meeting hall.  Large open-sided pavilions over the square give shade in summer and shelter from the winter rains, and serve the market on market days.  The town hall, which also houses the militia headquarters and the proconsul's office, stands on the square.  The town vault and the public library are in the same building or next door.  A vote that isn't urgent can be delayed until the next assembly meeting, so that the heads can confer with the other adults of their tables first.  The details of procedure are worked out by the first town's assembly, and later towns inherit its customs.

Most agricultural land is owned by the town and managed by the council.  Each table is given its own set of long-term responsibilities, such as tending a particular field.  Communal labor is also assigned at the table level, with whole tables rotated through public projects as units.  A crew assigned to a project is called a detail, and its members are "on detail."  This way, the head of a table is also the head of its work crew.  Most labor projects are scheduled around the tables' agricultural work.  To plan the rotations, the council keeps track of how many adults and children are in each table, and how many of the adults are on detail (which is the size of the work crew that table provides).  Children count as citizens for voting weight, although they aren't expected to do as much labor as adults, and are often excused from labor for school or apprenticeships.  Skilled craftsmen have separate arrangements, and are sometimes released from detail so they can practice their craft, paying taxes instead.

The majority of the harvest goes to the town, although tables are rewarded for doing a good job with their fields.  The form of the reward is worked out between the council and each table.  Staple food is stored in the town granary, and each table collects its allotted food from the granary as needed.  A table's food allowance is proportional to the number of its members who are on detail or excused (see Records); for members who are taxpayers, the table has to pay extra.  Children receive a reduced allowance based on their age.

Tables are also paid in tokens for some fraction of their work on detail, in proportion to how well they work.  This money can be spent on vegetables, clothing, luxuries, and so on.  Tables also do part-time crafts at home, such as spinning and weaving, and trade cloth and other goods with other households.  Spinning in particular is done across the whole nation, in spare moments, and yarn is a steady trade good: the ports buy it in bulk for sailcloth and rope.

There is no sales tax.  Grain is taxed at the harvest.  Other goods are taxed once, when they leave a town's gates for another town; this is the road tax merchants pay.  Local markets and trade between households are not taxed at all: that work is officially invisible.  (Foreign trade is handled completely differently, and is not specified here.)  A town pays more tax if it harvests more grain or sells more goods to other towns (in effect, since merchants pass the gate tax back to the sellers), but there is no disincentive for people to do any other kind of work.  The system is designed to reward towns for being self-sufficient, since whatever a town makes and uses itself is never taxed, while still allowing trade and specialization: goods traded between towns are taxed only once, so trade still pays wherever one town can make something much better or more cheaply than another.  Fruit and vegetables are less communal; they are bought and sold at a market held every four days, twice per 8-day week (see Records), and at a short market early every morning (see Civic Customs).

Membership in a table is flexible:

* **Immigrants** are "adopted" into a table.  This is how newcomers are brought into civic life.  There are no limits on immigration, but every new immigrant must have a table willing to sponsor them.  The nation may also let larger groups of refugees settle temporarily until they can be integrated.  Refugees camp outside the walls, like any other temporary camp; while the island is still filling, a refugee group can serve as the construction crew for the next planned town, joining its tables as it fills.  Because every immigrant needs a sponsoring table, and tables have a size limit, immigration naturally slows as the towns fill up, without any formal limit.
* **Returning colonists** with family in a town can easily join a table.
* **Marriage** — a newly married couple chooses which table to join, based on table size, work, personal conflicts, and so on.
* **Swapping** — anyone may switch tables if everyone involved agrees.  For example, a second cousin who wants to work in the orchards might move his household to the table responsible for them.

There may be a limit on the number of adults in a table, since tables that are too large would make the town's labor rotations too difficult to organize.  A table that grows too large splits in two.  Alternatively, members from several full tables may leave together to form a new table.  Small tables can also merge if needed.

**The table house.**  Each table lives in a courtyard house of about twenty people, with its rooms arranged around a central open courtyard where much of daily life takes place: a hall where the whole table eats together; a kitchen; family rooms, and small bedrooms upstairs; a storeroom and a workroom; a washroom and a latrine; and a cistern (see Appendix: The Table House).

After one town is established and proves successful, the design is copied for more towns; eventually we end up with many towns in federation.  New towns are founded by volunteers, whole tables at a time, either from the parent town or from the cities; if there aren't enough volunteers, lots are drawn among the parent town's tables and the city tables whose members are on detail.

A town plans a split when its walkable farmland can no longer be expanded and its surpluses start to get smaller.  Since preparing a new town takes decades, the council has to spot this trend early, rather than waiting for a shortfall; the town's records of granary intake, harvest taxes, and population trends make it visible year by year.  Planning for the new town begins decades before anyone moves in:

1. **Survey** of the site and its land.
2. **Planning** of the roads and town walls, then the town layout and land use.
3. **Orchards** are cleared and planted first, since they take years to mature.
4. **The road** from the parent town is built, along with irrigation and wells.
5. **Clearing** of the fields begins.
6. **Building** of the first houses and the essential public buildings, such as the granary, the mill, and the cesspits.
7. **Settlement.**  No families move in until the land is ready for the first plowing.

Most of this work is done by detail crews from the parent town.  Once a town is well established, with its irrigation system and buildings complete, some of its detail labor shifts to preparing the next town.  

What distinguishes these towns from cities are that they are agriculturally self-sufficient.  The fields, orchards, and pastures surrounding the town are sufficient to feed the entire population.  Of course, each town will specialize and trade.  At some point, both the efficiencies of trade and the requirements of coordinating the federation will require that a national capital city be built.  That is, a larger municipality that is dependent on trade with nearby towns for sustenance.  As a matter of policy, the city isn't allowed to grow beyond the needed size.  Ancient Rome, fed by grain shipped from Egypt and North Africa, shows the danger: its food supply became a weapon in the hands of whoever controlled it, and when the Vandals took North Africa, the city starved and shrank.

A city has its own city assembly and city council, but they operate differently from a town's.  A city can be thought of as having a town inside it: the people who work the city's local farms and local public works are organized much like a town.  The rest of the city's people are merchants, craftsmen, bureaucrats, and scholars who mostly work for themselves.  City life is therefore much less communal than town life, and there are almost two classes of citizens, based on the kind of work they do.

City people are still organized into tables, but most city tables exist only for representation, since their adults don't work together.  Even so, running a household is expensive and time-consuming, so most city tables do their domestic work together: cooking, cleaning, and child care.

## The Nation

The nation's capital is a small city supported by the towns' surplus.  It is responsible for maintaining roads and coordinating foreign policy.  In this case it is strategically located near many of the nation's most important mines.  In the sixth millennium HE, these are copper mines. 

After establishing the core of the nation and spending four centuries building better irrigation systems, they begin to look outward.  They build a second city, a port city located on the coast.  They build ships and begin trading with the outside world as well as defending their shores from pirates and invaders.  Eventually there may be three or four port cities, each facing a different part of the Mediterranean.

Over time, the port city becomes a major trading hub for the entire region, and they establish their university there, inviting potential scholars from across the known world to join them.  Since no other people has writing yet, these are not scholars in the usual sense: they are native speakers, craftsmen, and storytellers who come as guest scholars, teaching the university their languages and crafts, and who (or whose children) can be trained as scholars in the federation's sense themselves.

To keep the nation from fragmenting, a system of mixing populations is instituted.  Each town has a cadre of national administrators who check that taxes are paid, local roads are maintained, the town militia is properly trained, and records are kept.  These are recruited from other parts of the nation and rotated into these administration positions for limited terms, so that they don't feel that they can set up a fiefdom.  Each administrator serves a five-year term in a town, with the terms of a town's team staggered so they don't all change at once.  After a few rotations, administrators are usually given a job in one of the cities.  The leading administrator in each town is called the proconsul, and the proconsuls answer to the Directorate (see below).  In a city, each district has its own district proconsul, and the district proconsuls answer to a city proconsul.  City proconsul is a more prestigious job than town proconsul.

The mixing also works on another level, among ordinary citizens.  The nation encourages them to make occasional pilgrimages to festivals in distant parts of the nation, and militia groups are sometimes sent to train with another town for a time.

Parallel to administrators, there is a town council and mayor who handle most local government issues.  The council is much smaller than the town assembly and is elected by it (see Tables); its members are locals who do the day-to-day work of local governance.  The mayor is simply the elected head of the council.  The council and the administrators act as checks on each other's power.

At the national level, each town's assembly chooses a representative to the national congress.  A town's voting power in the congress is proportional to its population in the most recent census.  The census can be taken every year, since it is easy: each table knows its members, each council knows its tables, and so each level of government already knows how many citizens it has.  Cities are divided into districts, each sized so that its population is roughly comparable to an average town's.  Each district chooses its own representative, whose voting power is likewise proportional to the district's population.

This follows the "one man, one vote" principle without the work of redrawing district boundaries: instead of adjusting the boundaries to make each district the same size, each representative's vote is weighted by the number of people they represent.  The same rule applies at every level, from the heads in a town assembly to the representatives in congress.

The congress meets once a year in the capital.  Representatives serve one-year terms, up to a maximum of about twelve terms.  The congress (not the town assemblies) elects a much smaller council called the Directorate, which does the day-to-day work of national government, just as a town council does for its town.  The Directorate chooses its own head, who is the president of the nation.  Town councils and the Directorate have no fixed size; they are as large as they need to be.  (The other details of the national government are still to be worked out.)

So each town has three leading officials, each chosen by a different body:

* **The proconsul**, chosen by the Directorate.  An outsider who leads the national administrators in the town.
* **The mayor**, chosen by the town council.  The head of the council.
* **The representative**, chosen by the town assembly.  The town's voice in the national congress.

The mayor and the representative are both members of the town council ex officio.  When the representative is away at the national congress, the council meets without them.

There are also national courts, whose judges are appointed by the congress rather than the Directorate, which is too busy with day-to-day government.  The national courts handle matters beyond a single town, such as disputes between towns, cases involving national officers, and appeals from the town courts.

Councilors, mayors, governors, directors, and the president are all chosen annually, with no term limits.  (Heads are the exception: they are chosen and replaced informally, by their table's consensus.)  Representatives are also chosen annually, but are the exception, with their limit of about twelve terms.  Any of these can be recalled at any time by a two-thirds supermajority of the body that chose them: an assembly its councilors or its representative, a council its mayor, a provincial council its governor, the congress its directors, and the Directorate its president.

The first town serves as the capital until the capital city is built.  There are no national officers at all until the second town is founded, and even then there are very few; the national government only takes on real work as the number of towns grows.

National administrators and scribes and sailors are recruited from across the nation.  Citizens in every town can apply for federal service jobs, and the rules require that selection be based purely on merit, through a set of examinations.  The same national examinations also select the students who earn scholarships for tertiary education.

Every town also has professional posts: teachers, physicians, engineers, scribes, and librarians.  In the early generations, these are part-time work, held by people who still share in their tables' labor.  They are national-service positions, paid somewhat better than the equivalent work in a city, so that skilled people flow back to the towns rather than collecting in the cities.  Once tertiary education becomes formalized, most of the jobs available to new graduates are in these posts, so a typical professional career begins in a town.

The nation explicitly refuses to expand beyond Cyprus, to keep its foreign policy simple.  People are free to emigrate and found colonies overseas, but those colonies are on their own: they are not part of the federation and are not defended by it.

Once the island is full, around 5600 HE, national policy shifts to encouraging embassies, trading posts, and colonies abroad.  Emigration then balances births and immigration.  Refugee groups who can't be absorbed at home can be helped to resettle overseas, near an embassy or outpost.

Many colonies probably begin as trading outposts: a few merchants and their families living among a foreign people, who are later joined by settlers as the outpost grows.

Protecting outposts is a complicated issue.  Where the nation has an official embassy, it naturally has marines guarding it, and merchants can attach themselves to the embassy for protection.  Elsewhere, merchants take their own risks.

Colonies will likely follow one of two paths.  Clusters of nearby colonies may form federations of their own, modeled on the one at home.  Others may become vassals of whatever local power rules the land around them.  Either way, the colonies naturally become trading hubs for the federation.  Trade privileges for colonies are decided case by case.  The rules are kept simple: any foreigner can trade at the ports and use tax tokens there, and any foreigner has legal standing in the courts.  The privileges a colony can earn are the right to send its cargo inland, beyond the port, and access to goods that are restricted for foreigners by default.  Colonists who want to come home can do so easily if they have family in a town, since they can join their family's table.

### Provinces

Between the towns and the nation is the province.  Each city has exactly one province: the set of towns more closely linked to that city than to any other, mostly through trade, since a city depends on the towns around it for its food.  The capital's province is the towns that feed the capital; each port city's province is the towns that send their surplus to that port.  Provinces exist only once there is more than one city: until the first port city is founded, there is no provincial level at all, and the national government handles everything directly.  With the capital and three or four ports, there are four or five provinces, each with a dozen to twenty towns.

Province boundaries follow trade, not lines on a map, so they are reviewed from time to time, and always when a new city is founded.  The evidence comes from the road tax: since goods are taxed when they leave a town's gates for another town, the tax records show where each town's goods go.  A town is assigned to whichever city takes the most of its trade, and a town that trades heavily with two cities may ask to be reassigned.

Provinces don't do much in the federation.  The island is small, and most government happens in the towns or in the capital.  But a few things are best handled at the provincial level: the roads between the province's towns and its city, the city's food supply and the emergency grain reserves, the mail circuits that meet at the city, and militia support between neighboring towns.  The representatives of the province's towns and city districts, already chosen for the national congress, also meet together as a provincial council, with votes weighted by population in the usual way.  The provincial council elects a governor as its head, just as a town council elects a mayor.  Alongside the governor, the Directorate appoints a prefect for each province: an outsider, rotated like the proconsuls, who leads the national administrators working at the provincial level.  The governor and the prefect check each other the way a mayor and a proconsul do.  (The other details of provincial government are still to be worked out.)

The province matters more as the constitution is adapted elsewhere.  If colonies on the mainland form confederations of their own, modeled on the one at home, a large state could have dozens of provinces, too many towns for a single congress to know well, and too far apart for a single capital to manage their roads and food supply.  There, the province becomes a real level of government, and the founders' constitution is written so that it can grow into one.  If a federation grows larger still, its provinces are grouped into larger sets in the same way, each built around the largest of its cities, and the levels can keep nesting as far as the state's size requires.

### Citizenship

There are three levels of membership in the nation:

* **Foreigners** have full legal rights and protection under the law, and can trade at the ports, but pay full taxes and buy their own food.  They cannot lease land, so they rent rooms and shops from citizens, live in a port's foreign quarter, or stay in temporary camps.
* **Provisional citizens** are immigrants who have been adopted by a table.  They live as members of the table, sharing its food allowance, detail work, and pay, and they serve in the militia.  When they are adopted, they take a provisional oath in public, promising to obey the laws, serve in the militia, and do their share for their table and town.  Their citizenship becomes permanent after some number of years, when they take the full citizenship oath, the same one young citizens take when they come of age (see Civic Customs).
* **Citizens** have, in addition: the right to vote, hold office, and sit on juries; the right to lease town land for a house or business; the right to take the federal service examinations; free schooling for their children; and the right, as a table, to sponsor immigrants.  In exchange for a lifetime of militia service, citizens pay slightly lower taxes than foreigners.

The right to citizenship is passed on to children in two ways: by blood, to the child of a citizen wherever the child is born, and by birth, to any child born in the nation, even to foreign parents.  But the right is not the same as citizenship itself, which requires civic participation: belonging to a table, serving in the militia, and doing one's share of detail work or paying taxes.  A child raised in a table grows into citizenship naturally.  Someone who holds the right but has never taken it up, such as a colonist's child raised overseas or a child born in a port's foreign quarter, lives as a foreigner until they claim it by joining a table, and doesn't need to go through the provisional period that immigrants do.

Not every citizen lives in a town or city, so not every citizen belongs to an ordinary table.  Citizens whose work keeps them away have their own tables under separate rules.  In the navy, for example, the navy is a sailor's table: sailors are grouped into tables of their own, separate from the officers, and their votes go through those tables.  Government employees serving overseas, such as embassy staff, are grouped into tables the same way, and so are rangers and officers.

The heads of these extra tables form the **assembly-at-large**.  It works like any other assembly, with votes weighted by each table's members, but since its members are scattered across the island and overseas, all of its voting and census-taking is done by mail, which is slow and tedious.  The assembly-at-large elects its own representative to the congress, who is usually someone living in the capital anyway, since that is easier.

## Records

In the ancient world, records were kept on paper, stone, and clay.  Almost all paper (papyrus) from the ancient world has long since deteriorated.  Carving words into stone is hard.  But the cuneiform tablets that the ancient Mesopotamians used have proved to be incredibly durable.  Therefore, all permanent records are written on fired clay tablets, while paper is used for records that don't need to survive more than a decade.

### Writing

Before traveling back in time, the time travelers devise an alphabet that works well on both clay and paper: the letters are designed to be legible whether made with a stylus on clay or with a pen on paper.  The book that defines the alphabet shows the canonical shape of every letter in two forms: one for impressing or cutting (in clay, wood, or stone) and one for writing in ink.  Each glyph gets a large illustration showing all of its detail, such as stroke order, proportions, and the angle of the stylus or pen.  The alphabet has about 30 characters, plus numerals and punctuation, not too many for an individual to learn.  Additionally, there is a full set of diacritic marks, which can be added to the letters to represent sounds as precisely as the IPA does.  The diacritics can be left off for most day-to-day use, but is helpful when writing a foreign name or documenting what the local language sounds like.  The travelers also devise a simplified "archival language" which is to be used for long-term common documentation.  It has simplified syntax, no idioms, and can be learned with the relatively small dictionary. This  language doesn't use any sounds requiring diacritic marks.   A language based off of the archival language, but including extra vocabulary, idioms, and flexible syntax is used as the day-to-day language, but it is expected to naturally drift over time.

The book that defines the alphabet also assigns every character a number from 0 to 255 (0x00 to 0xFF), so that any text can be sent as a sequence of numbers.  The basic set, 0 to 63 (0x00 to 0x3F), holds the letters, the digits, the word divider, and basic punctuation, plus an escape code.  This basic set is what is used for signaling, by semaphore or heliograph (see Signaling).  Diacritics are not needed for basic signaling.  The numbers from 64 to 255 (0x40 to 0xFF) hold the diacritics, the paired marks, paragraph marks, and extra symbols (such as mathematical symbols), with room left for future use.

Words are separated not by a blank space but by a tiny interpunct (a raised dot), which is part of the basic set.  Besides quotation marks and parentheses, there are paired marks that enclose text to show emphasis, loudness, or a proper name.  The alphabet has only one case, and the use of bold, uppercase, or italic letter forms is discouraged; emphasis and the like are always shown with these explicit marks instead.  That way, the meaning survives in every medium: impressed in clay, written in ink, or sent by signal.

The same alphabet and diacritics are used to write down foreign languages.  A linguist who learns a foreign language can record it in letters that any other trained linguist can read and pronounce correctly.  Each language has its own "mode": the alphabet written out with each letter marked with its default diacritic for that language.  In everyday writing, a letter with its default diacritic is written bare, and only departures from the default are marked.  The full diacritics are kept for foreign words and names, when a word from one language is written within another language's mode.

In each town, universal basic literacy is encouraged, and a small school is established.  Every child is taught how to make a clay tablet using river mud, the alphabet, and how to read and write their own language.  Older children are taught the archival language, which is used for contracts, laws, and communication across long distances.  (Like Latin in the middle ages, but many times easier to learn).  Students who show promise are recruited to be scribes, scholars, or administrators.

### Paper and Books

Paper is made locally from the start, but remains expensive. It is used for documents that don't need to last more than a decade or for documents that must be shipped long distances.

Paper is pulp paper, made from flax, reeds, and eventually linen rags.  The hardest part of papermaking is beating the fiber into pulp, so paper mills use water-powered hammers wherever there is a stream, and treadmills (turned by people or oxen) elsewhere.

Paper documents are bound as books rather than rolled as scrolls, so that both sides of each sheet can be used and any page can be found quickly.

Every book begins with a title page giving its title, its author, the language it is written in, the date and place it was written, and a short summary in the archival language.  Books in obscure languages also give that language's mode on the title page, so that any trained reader can pronounce the text correctly.  Every copy adds the name of the copyist, the date and place of copying, the copy it was made from, and its copy depth (the number of copying steps between it and the original), so that the history of every text can be traced.

Every book is divided into standard pages, and every copy, on paper or clay, keeps the same page breaks as the original, so that a page number refers to the same text in every copy.  The foot of every page gives the page number, the total number of pages, and the number of characters on the page, so that any copy can be checked page by page.  Copies are made from a checked master and proofread by one person reading aloud while another follows the copy; important numbers are written in both digits and words, doubts are noted in the margin rather than silently corrected, and errors found in a master are reported to the national vaults (see Documentation) so that every library can be corrected.

Most writing is done by hand, but books that are needed in many copies, such as school primers, the ethics books, the laws, and the yearly calendar of moons, are printed with movable type.  Printing on clay is easy: individual letters, carved in wood or fired in clay, are set in a frame and pressed into a wet tablet, with no ink and no press.  Printing on paper takes more: metal or fired-clay type, an oil-based ink that clings to the type, and a screw press adapted from the olive and wine presses.  Standard page breaks make typesetting simple, since every page is set exactly as it appears in the master copy.  Printing also changes proofreading: instead of checking every copy, the proofreaders check one proof of each typeset page against the master, and every copy printed from it has the same copy depth.

The book on writing and records gives recipes for several inks, each suited to a different purpose: carbon ink for everyday writing, iron gall ink for records that must not be altered, oil-based printing ink, red ink for headings and corrections, seal ink, and slip paint for labeling clay tablets (see Appendix: Inks).

### Calendar and Time

Days are counted from the New Year, the northern-hemisphere winter solstice, which is day 0, and years are counted in the Holocene Era (HE), so that scholars can discuss events long before the arrival without negative numbers.  A date is written YYYY-DDD, in hexadecimal: a four-digit year and a three-digit day number.  For example, 1518-0B6 is the year 5400 HE (0x1518), day 182 (the summer festival; see Civic Customs).

In practice, the solstice doesn't need to be observed every year.  Astronomers predict it, and leap years follow a predictable 33-year cycle (8 leap years in every 33), which matches the solar year very closely.  When observations show the calendar has drifted, the cycle is adjusted as needed.  This keeps distant towns in agreement even through cloudy winters.

A week is half the base of the number system: 8 days.  Since days are simply numbered from the solstice, the week falls out of the day number: every day number ending in hex digit 0 or 8 starts a new week.  The weeks restart at each New Year, making this a perpetual calendar: a given day number always falls on the same day of the week, every year.  The 365 days of the year make 45 full weeks plus a short final week of 5 days (6 in a leap year).

Since the moon's phases don't fit evenly into the solar year, they are left out of the perpetual calendar.  Instead, the astronomers publish a calendar each year listing the days of the new and full moons, so that anyone who needs them, such as fishermen, sailors, and night travelers, can plan around them.  The same calendar can give approximate sunrise and sunset each week of the year.

The day is divided into 16 hours of 90 (of our) minutes each, and each hour into 16 degrees, so that the smallest everyday unit, the degree, is 1/256 of a day, about five and a half of our minutes.  (It is called a degree because the sky turns one degree of angle in that time.)  That is precise enough for any practical scheduling; nobody arranges a meeting more precisely than five minutes.  Two further divisions by 16 give the second, 1/65536 of a day, about 1.3 of our seconds (see Appendix: Units).

Since the day is divided into powers of 16, a time of day is just written as a fractional day: .80 is noon, and .C004 is four seconds after 6 p.m.  A moment in time adds the fraction to the date, with as many digits as needed, written YYYY-DDD[.HM[SS]]; for example, 1518-0B6.C0 is 6 p.m. on that summer festival day.  The day begins at midnight, which is the most consistent choice: a day counted from sunrise would suit work schedules, but would change length through the year.  Midnight is found as exactly half a day after noon, which the sundial marks.  For now, each town keeps its own local time; noon in the east of the island comes about 9 minutes before noon in the west, too small to matter.

Each town keeps time with a sundial for setting noon and a water clock for keeping time through the night and on cloudy days.  A public timekeeper sounds the daytime hours on a horn.

### Money

In the founding years, life is fully communal and there is no currency.  Tokens and taxes are introduced by the first town's council once the economy becomes complex enough to need them.  After that, most people exchange "tax tokens" which are ceramic coins with a unique glaze and imprint.  These have value because the state only accepts tax payments with them.  There is a token for each factor of 4: 0x1, 0x4, 0x10, 0x40, 0x100, and so on.

Counterfeiting is guarded against by a single, guarded national mint at the capital; secret glaze recipes; fine imprints from carved dies; dated series and changing designs; inspection of every token as it comes back in taxes; and severe punishment.

Communal labor is itself a kind of tax.  Every adult is either on detail (doing communal labor), excused (disabled, retired, or on parental leave), or taxpaying.  Public servants count as taxpaying, but rather than paying taxes, they are simply paid less, which saves accounting.  Adult students are paid by the state to study.  Anyone who can't pay their taxes simply goes on detail instead.  Citizens who don't do communal labor, such as most city merchants, craftsmen, and scholars, pay taxes in tokens instead.  Agricultural towns sell a portion of their output, mostly to the cities, to earn the tokens they need to pay national taxes.  This keeps tokens circulating: the cities pay towns for food, and both pay the tokens back to the state.

New tokens are created by a national lending bank, which brings them into circulation by making loans.  If a borrower dies, the loan is paid from their estate, or else it is defaulted on; it is never passed on to their heirs as debt.  The bank is guided by one of the small number of books the original travelers brought with them: a simplified explanation of macroeconomics.

The bank is run by the Board of Macroeconomics, which is selected by the Directorate and ratified by the congress.  The Directorate is also allowed to create new tokens directly, but with the understanding that the Board must account for them in its own policy.

The Board and the Directorate understand what the tax system is really for.  Collecting taxes from the towns is mostly a mechanism for making the towns sell food to the cities.  They also understand that high inflation makes markets behave badly: if tokens lose value, towns have less reason to sell, and there could be less food in the cities.  Keeping the currency stable is ultimately about keeping the cities fed.

If the optimal level of inflation is ever worked out (perhaps 2% or 3% a year), the Board can target it.  Prices then rise slowly and predictably, so every so many years the smallest token is retired and a new, largest denomination is created.  At 2% inflation, a step of 4 between token sizes means retiring one token roughly every 70 years, about once in a lifetime.

There is no foreign trade in the early centuries.  Much later, once the port is built and foreign trade begins, the mint strikes trade coins of copper, and later of silver and gold, each worth only its weight in metal.  They come in two masses, 1/16 jug (about 79 g) and 1/256 jug (one spoon, about 4.9 g), and are struck thick so that they wear slowly.

### Mail

There is a limited mail service, run by a handful of postmen, all of them rangers, who travel from town to town in fixed circuits.  They visit only the town hall of each town, and only every few weeks; as the nation grows more cosmopolitan, the service becomes more regular.

To send a letter, a citizen addresses it to "NAME / TOWNSHIP" or "NAME / TABLE NUMBER / TOWNSHIP" and leaves it at the proconsul's office for collection.  When a postman arrives with mail, a horn call tells the town that the mail has come.  Individuals then find out about their letters in a haphazard way; towns are small enough not to need anything better.

Most circuits meet at the capital, the ports, or other hub towns, where postmen hand off letters to one another.  The assembly-at-large, whose voting and census-taking are done entirely by mail, depends on this system.

## Military

The town militia is composed of the majority of the adult population. (Pregnant women are put on indefinite leave, old people are moved into advisory roles.  Children are trainees.)  They train for an afternoon every week or so; this is a community event.

Everyone is trained on the basic weapons they are expected to maintain: a sharpened staff, a shield, a bow or a sling, and an axe.  Learning to make these weapons oneself is part of the training.   Bronze spears and helmets, if they are available, are kept in the town armory.  Once the iron age arrives, iron is far cheaper than bronze, and as soon as the town can afford it, the stone weapons are replaced: every militia member is issued an iron knife, spear tip, axe head, and helmet to keep at home, so that arms stay spread through the population rather than locked in an armory.  Militia members must bring all of their weapons and gear, in good condition, to every weekly practice.

In the event that they are called up to send reinforcements to a nearby town, only a small force of a couple hundred at most are sent, mostly composed of young men.  Women's military role is often in the archery corps, which is less useful for a rapid-response force but very useful for fighting from the town walls.  Older men are also more likely to stay behind.  Since a relief force is small, most of the militia, including most women and older men, remains at home to hold the walls while it is away.

The town militia also serves as a fire-fighting and emergency response force.  It is led by a militia commander, who is either the proconsul or someone the proconsul appoints.  Only two or three of the militia's officers are part of the national administration cadre; the rest of the militia, officers and ranks alike, are local citizens.

The militia is not organized by tables, since members of the same family often serve in different roles (archers, spearmen, and so on).  Instead, it is organized into squads and companies of its own.  One company is always "on call": its members carry their weapons with them to work, and answer a distress call immediately.

The militia uses a standard system of horn signals, the same in every town.  Signals might include:

* Fire.
* On-call company assemble.
* All militia assemble.
* Everyone assemble (all citizens, not just the militia).
* Even-numbered (or odd-numbered) companies assemble.
* Assemble by company along the walls.
* The mail has arrived.

If one town is attacked, nearby towns send militia detachments to support it.  Having a commander from outside the town matters most in exactly this case.  The commander can order a company of militia to go to its aid at once.  A council might dither over sending its own people away, but a single outsider can make the decision quickly.  The militia will usually obey, because they know the other town would do the same for them, for the same reasons.

The cities' militia has a better organized core, a professional guard unit.  These are recruited from town militias from across the countryside.  On the military side of the career path, the head of a city guard might be promoted to militia general.  The militia generals write the uniform training guidelines for all militias and guards.  The port cities have a standing navy (with marine corps), which patrols the nearby waters to deter and respond to coastal piracy.  In the event of an armed invasion, the local town militias will hold their town walls while the navy and marines arrive to relieve the siege and intercept the invader's ships.

As the only large, mobile, full-time force, the navy is the most likely source of a coup.  Possible safeguards include rotating naval commanders regularly, recruiting marines from the town militias for limited terms so that they return home afterward, and splitting the fleet among several squadrons or bases with separate commanders.  Each of these has to be balanced against efficiency: experienced commanders, long-serving crews, and a unified fleet make for a better navy.

Towns have guard patrols, but those are just members of the militia who are rotated into that role.  Criminal investigation is handled by part-time magistrates appointed by the council and national administrators, who serve as both detectives and prosecutors, with the council acting as a panel of judges.  This overlap is deliberate, a different kind of separation of powers: the guards are local citizens, but the militia they belong to is commanded by the national side (only two or three of its officers are part of the national administration cadre), while the magistrates are appointed jointly and the council sits in judgment, so neither side controls law enforcement alone.

### Rangers

There is a final military group, the rangers: something between a frontier marshal and a park ranger.  Their territory is everything outside the town fields: the national forest, with its roads, hills, mountains, and coast between towns.

Their duties include:

* **Law on the roads**: dealing with bandits and thieves, and protecting travelers and merchants.
* **Arrests**: catching suspects in the wild and bringing them to the nearest town's magistrate.  Rangers investigate and detain, but the towns judge.
* **Forest stewardship**: forests are public property, protected by stewardship laws, and rangers enforce them in the national forest, as well as monitoring where cutting outpaces replanting.
* **Fire watch**: spotting the summer wildfires that are a serious danger on Cyprus.
* **The relay stations**: staffing, guarding, and maintaining the heliograph stations on the remote peaks.
* **Search and rescue, and guiding**: finding lost shepherds, and escorting scholars and surveyors.
* **The last independent locals**: in the early generations, rangers are the ones who meet the island's remaining unabsorbed bands.
* **Coast watch**: spotting raiders or foreigners landing away from the ports.
* **The mail**: a handful of rangers serve as postmen, traveling fixed circuits between town halls (see Mail).

The rangers are a national force, answering to the national military command (the militia generals), since their territory belongs to no single town.  They are recruited from the town militias through the merit examinations, and are rotated between regions, like the national administrators, so they don't become local strongmen in remote areas.  They work alone or in pairs, on foot; there are no horses yet, though donkeys, once imported, make good pack animals.

### Signaling

Long-distance messages are sent by heliograph: a polished copper or brass mirror flashing sunlight, visible for 30–50 km in clear weather.  A station needs only a few kilograms of copper for its mirrors, but the mirrors must be polished daily, and they get brighter once tin makes better mirror alloys possible.  A chain of relay stations on the high peaks carries messages across the island.

Not every town has its own relay mirror, but every town has a watchtower set up to receive messages.  Each town's watchtower is staffed by the militia, rotated like the guard patrols, with a few trained signalers in every company; messages are logged and passed to the proconsul's office.  Short-range signals, such as between a town and a nearby relay station, along the walls, or between ships, use semaphore flags.  At night, beacons or lamps are used instead.

## Heraldry

The travelers introduce flags and standardized heraldry early on.  Every town already has a seal, which appears on its documents, obituary tablets, and citizens' medallions (see Documentation).  A town's heraldry is simply a color version of its seal, used on standards, banners, painted walls, and militia sashes.

The first standards are carved emblems on poles; cloth banners come later, as weaving and dyeing improve.

Heraldic designs follow a small set of rules, so that they can be reproduced in any medium:

* **Simple shapes** that work as a clay stamp, a painted wall, a woven sash, or a banner.
* **Readable without color**, since clay seals are monochrome.  Each color has a standard hatching pattern, so a design can be fully described in a clay impression.
* **A small palette** of local pigments and dyes: white (undyed linen), black (soot), red (ochre or madder), yellow (ochre), and brown (umber, which Cyprus is famous for).
* **A registry**: every design is recorded on clay in the national vaults, and no two towns may share one.

There is a national flag, and each town has its own design.  Tables may have simple marks of their own.

There are no uniforms; cloth is far too expensive for that.  Instead, militia members wear a sash or armband in their town's colors, so that a relief force from another town can be recognized at a distance.  Officers wear a distinguishing mark, such as a sash of a different color.  The ceramic medallion every citizen already wears serves as an identity tag.

## Documentation

Merchant ships dispatched from the ports often go on exploration missions, carrying scholars with them who can document the people (local languages and customs) and geography (potential resources) they encounter, while the merchants look for new trade opportunities.  With the founders' tools, a single scholar can enter a remote village and write down the basics of its language with the phonetic alphabet, its oral traditions in the archival language, and its songs in musical notation.  The documents are sent back to the university to fulfill the second core goal of the nation. 

To make the process of documenting history concrete to every citizen, the obituary serves as both a death certificate and a piece of history.  When a person dies, their family composes a tablet recording the facts of their life and a list of their accomplishments.  The town administrators check it, and it is sealed and fired in three copies: one for the family vault, one for the town vault, and one for the national vaults (see Appendix: Obituaries).

A second small copy of the birth certificate is kept by each individual to serve as an identifying "signature" in their life.  This is worn as a ceramic medallion;  it has the town seal of the issuing town as well as an impression of the citizen's thumbprint. 

The national vaults are only begun once the capital is established.  Records from before then exist only in the town vaults and family vaults, so anyone researching the early centuries has to go town by town.  In practice, only genealogists will bother.

Old documents in the town and national vaults will often be poorly organized, maybe only going as far as keeping each year in a separate stack.  This is okay; the intention is to enable future researchers with more time on their hands to index the data.

## Society

The society is seeded with more modern moral values and laws.  Equity under the law, democratic accountability, no slavery or inherited debt.  Whether these all "stick" is unknown, but that's the point of the experiment.

Furthermore, the idea of sustainability is a fundamental feature of the cultural code.  It is a rule of ethics, taught in every school: if a tree is chopped down, another should be planted to make up for it; if water is not used sustainably, something needs to change.  Because forests are publicly owned, the rule also becomes law there: the forests are protected by stewardship laws.

The founders' default laws criminalize:

* **Against persons:** murder (including infanticide, human sacrifice, and killing in blood feuds), manslaughter, assault and maiming, rape, kidnapping, torture, slavery, forced marriage, and child abuse and neglect.
* **Against property:** robbery (including cattle raiding and piracy), theft, fraud, extortion, and vandalism (including arson).
* **Against animals:** animal cruelty.
* **Against the records and the currency:** forgery of records, seals, or medallions; counterfeiting; and deliberate destruction of archives.
* **Against the government:** bribery and corruption, abuse of office, perjury, election and census fraud, and treason and armed rebellion.
* **Against public health:** fouling water supplies, and knowingly spreading disease or breaking a quarantine.

Militia offenses, such as desertion or refusing a lawful call to arms, are covered by a separate set of rules that applies only to citizens.

Punishment for crimes is most often a sentence of penal labor: assignment to a penal crew, away from one's family, for a set period of time.  Prison and capital punishment also exist, but the society can't afford to run large prisons, so prison is used sparingly.

The default laws also set basic rules for how the government must enforce the law.  These rights apply equally to citizens and foreigners:

* Punishment must be proportional to the crime.
* Capital punishment must be as quick as possible.
* Magistrates, judges, and jurors must act without bias.
* The accused is presumed innocent; the accuser must prove guilt.
* The accused must be told the charges and allowed to speak in their own defense.
* No one may be punished for an act that was not a crime when it was done.
* The law binds officials too, including proconsuls, prefects, governors, directors, and the president.
* A confession obtained by force is void.
* Judges should follow precedent, deciding similar cases in similar ways, but should correct earlier decisions when they turn out to be wrong.

Juries are used only for capital crimes outside the military.  Only serious cases have their trials recorded. 

There are almost no laws about marriage and family structure.  The founders bring their own ideas about family, and early immigrants will likely set many of the customs as they adapt to those ideas.

## Religion and Ethics

The travelers take a relaxed attitude toward religion.  They neither discourage it nor invent a religion of their own; people are free to keep the beliefs they bring with them or develop new ones.

Ethics, on the other hand, is taught to everyone, in the same schools that teach reading and writing.  People are raised to think of ethics as separate from religion: something shared by all citizens whatever they believe.

The founders' ethics texts are really several books, aimed at different groups: simple stories for young children, harder cases for older students, and books for adults, such as future heads, judges, and officers.  They teach through stories and questions rather than rules and theory.  Each short chapter takes one principle, illustrates it with a story (a fable for young children, a realistic situation for older ones), and ends with questions for the class to discuss.  The core principles include:

* Treat others as you would want to be treated.
* Every person has equal worth: no one owns another, debts are not inherited, and foreigners are people too.
* Do no harm, to people or to animals.  (This explains *why* the basic laws forbid what they do.)
* Tell the truth and keep your promises.
* Care for those who can't care for themselves: children, the sick, and the old.
* Do your share, as fairness to your neighbors rather than obedience.
* A tree for a tree: don't take more than you give back.
* Be fair even when you have power.
* Think for yourself, look at the evidence, admit your mistakes, and respect people who believe differently.

The books for older readers add two ideas.  First, the law is the minimum, and ethics asks more: lying to a friend isn't illegal, but it is still wrong.  Second, there are several ways to judge an action: by its consequences, by duties and rights, and by what a good person would do.  The hardest cases, where two good principles conflict, are left open for students to argue out.

The closest thing to a state religion is the custom of declaring "national heroes."  After a person dies, they may be named a national hero by the congress (the original travelers are treated like everyone else, and can be named too), and people are encouraged to admire and remember the good things they did while alive.  By custom, no one is named a national hero until at least 20 years after their death, so that the decision is made with some perspective rather than in the grief or enthusiasm of the moment.  The obituary tablets, with their lists of accomplishments, are the natural record for choosing them.

### Civic Customs

Alongside the national heroes, the society has a set of non-religious customs for the major events of life and the year.  They are designed so that believers never feel any of it goes against their religion, while still giving enough ceremony to satisfy someone with no religion at all.  The customs are about community, memory, and gratitude to people; they make no claims about gods.  Each includes a moment of silence, which believers can use for prayer and others for reflection, and anyone is free to add their own religious rites alongside.  Most are led by the head of the table, or by a councilor for town-wide events.

* **Birth.**  The child is presented to the table and named.  The birth tablet is written, and the child's thumbprint is pressed into a medallion.
* **Coming of age.**  A young person joins the militia, attends a first weekly inspection with weapons they made themselves, and writes their first tablet in the archival language.  They then take the citizenship oath in public, promising to uphold the constitution and the laws, to serve in the militia, and to do their share for the town and the nation, and become full citizens.
* **Marriage.**  The marriage is announced in the public square, and the couple announces which table they will join.  The table welcomes them, and the whole community is invited to celebrate with them.  The marriage is recorded on clay.
* **Death.**  The family composes the obituary, reads it aloud at the table, and watches as it is fired in the kiln.  The family copy is placed in the family vault.  The burial is public: the community joins a procession to the town cemetery, which lies outside the walls, well away from any source of drinking water.

There is a major festival every 91 days, so four a year, falling close to the solstices and equinoxes:

* **Day 0: New Year**, at the winter solstice, which begins the calendar's count of days.
* **Day 91: Spring festival**, near the spring equinox.
* **Day 182: Summer festival**, near the summer solstice.  On Cyprus it falls just after the grain harvest, so it is held at the town granary, giving thanks to the people whose work filled it; elsewhere, it can be adapted to the local harvest.
* **Day 273: Autumn festival**, near the autumn equinox, before the autumn rains and the sowing.

Four 91-day quarters make 364 days, leaving one extra day at the end of the year, or two in a leap year, which can be festival days themselves leading into the New Year.  Heroes' Day, an annual remembrance of the national heroes, falls at one of the four festivals.  In the southern hemisphere, where the seasons are reversed, people celebrate the festivals on the same days, but may rename them to match their own seasons.

Town life also follows a daily and weekly rhythm.  At this level of technology, nobody can spare many whole days from work, so most customs take only part of a day:

* **Evenings.**  Every evening, when the weather permits, the town gathers in the square after the day's work: for music, dancing, drinking, games, gossip, and courting, or just to sit and talk with neighbors from other tables.
* **Assembly.**  The heads of the tables gather in the square to learn their tables' detail assignments, and the assembly meets at the same time (see Tables).
* **Markets.**  The main market is held every four days, twice a week, and only in the morning, so that everyone can get back to work afterward.  A short market is also held early every morning, for the freshest food, such as fish, milk, greens, and soft fruit.  Since nobody lives more than a four-minute walk from the square, a quick trip to market costs almost no time.  A market stall can be as simple as a wheelbarrow or handcart, loaded at the house and rolled out to the square each morning, then rolled home again when the market ends.
* **Militia practice.**  One afternoon a week, the militia drills in the yard.  It is a community event: families come to watch, and children train alongside the adults.
* **Free day.**  In the off season, when the fields need less work, such as midwinter after the sowing or high summer after the threshing, the council may declare a weekly free day, if the town can afford it.

## Timeline

### How many towns can Cyprus support?

Cyprus is about 9,250 km², but much of it is mountain (the Troodos massif and the Kyrenia range).  Roughly half the island is good lowland farmland, mostly the central Mesaoria plain and the coastal plains.

Five kilometers is a long walk to make twice a day, so a town's fields all lie within about 3 km of its walls, and only the farthest pastures reach out to about 5 km.  Including its pastures, each town still uses roughly 60–80 km².  Packing those territories across the farmland gives about **60–80 full-size towns**, plus a number of smaller towns in the hills.  The fields within 3 km (about 28 km²) can feed a town of roughly two to two and a half thousand people, so the total population is roughly **120,000–200,000** once every town has filled, plus the cities.  This matches Cyprus's pre-industrial population in other periods (the 1881 census counted about 186,000), so it is a reasonable ceiling.

### Growth

Year 0 is the arrival, 5000 HE.  A generation is about 25 years.

The first town grows from a few hundred travelers to its full size of about 2,500, roughly an eightfold increase.  About a hundred locals join each generation.  Better hygiene and medicine mean far fewer children die young than in neighboring societies, so the population grows rapidly.  With good medicine and nutrition, natural growth of about 2% a year, plus these locals, gets the town to full size in about four generations.  (Without the locals, it would take about five.)

After that, new towns are founded when existing ones fill up, and the number of towns doubles about every three generations (about 75 years).  Labor spent on walls, irrigation, and roads keeps growth slower than the population could manage.  In these early centuries, the only immigrants available are the island's own locals.

Population grows more slowly than the number of towns suggests, because at any moment about half the towns are newly founded and still filling up.  The average town holds about 1,550 people during the expansion, and the total keeps rising toward its ceiling for a few generations after the last town is founded.

The timing of the capital, port, and university is provisional.

Once the island begins to feel full, birth rates are expected to fall on their own, as they did in our own demographic transition: children survive, education is universal (including for girls), and old age doesn't depend on having many children, since the table and the granary support those who are excused.  The state encourages the transition gently, never by coercion:

* The medicine book covers birth spacing and family planning.
* The sustainability ethic is applied openly to population: don't take more than the land can give.
* More young women are encouraged into better jobs, such as scholarship, national service, and skilled crafts, that require delaying marriage.
* Emigration abroad remains the release valve.

The port is founded about two centuries before the island fills up.  By the time there is no more farmland for new towns, the federation already has ships, sea routes, and experience abroad.  Exploration and trade then become the outlet for extra population: people who can't find a place at home go to sea as sailors, traders, scholars, and settlers.  Settlements founded overseas are independent; they do not become part of the nation (see The Nation).

For comparison: in our history, Mesopotamia is in the Ubaid period at this time, the first true cities of the Uruk period appear around 6000 HE, and Egypt is unified around 6900 HE.  The federation fills Cyprus before the first cities of the mainland exist.

### Contact with the mainland

At the time of the arrival, there is no Mesopotamian or Egyptian civilization yet, only farming towns and villages.  Southern Mesopotamia is in the Ubaid period, with temple towns such as Eridu and early irrigation canals.  Egypt has scattered farming villages along the Nile, such as Merimde in the delta.  Neither has writing or cities.  When contact comes, the federation is the most advanced society on Earth.

But this is only what archaeology can show.  The travelers' knowledge of the period is patchy: they know where sites like Eridu are and what was dug up there, but not how those people lived, what languages they spoke, who ruled them, or whether they kept slaves.  Much of what the explorers find will be a surprise, and documenting it is the whole point of the second goal.

Likely contact dates:

* **From the start, informally; formally by 5400 HE** — The Levant and Anatolian coasts, 70–100 km away and visible from Cyprus's mountains.  The local Cypriots already make this crossing.
* **5100–5200 HE** — Small scholarly expeditions by paddled boat to the nearby mainland, to begin documenting its peoples.
* **~5425–5475 HE** — Northern Mesopotamia, reached overland from the Syrian coast (about 200 km to the Euphrates).
* **~5450–5500 HE** — Egypt, about 400 km by sea, once real sailing ships exist.
* **~5500 HE** — Southern Mesopotamia and the Ubaid heartland, more than 1,000 km down the Euphrates.

## Story Ideas

* **First contact**, from either side.
* **An immigrant's point of view**: joining a table, learning the archival language, adapting to the founders' customs.
* **Early exploration** of a world we know very little about.
* **Where the constitution doesn't work** as well as hoped.
* **Where the constitution is changed**, for good or bad.
* **Cultural conflict**, at home and abroad.
* **The ethics of trading with a slaveholding society.**
* **The case for empire.**  A faction argues against the no-expansion rule: the federation's institutions are better than anything else in the world, so spreading them by conquest or annexation would do the most good.  Opponents answer that empire is exactly the path the founders came back to avoid.
* **A twisted copy.**  A foreign power adopts parts of the federation's technology and culture, but turns them into something very different.  For example:
  * A temple elite adopts the alphabet and clay archives, but keeps literacy for priests only and uses the records to track debts and labor owed.
  * The census, birth tablets, and thumbprint medallions become a way to register slaves and conscripts.
  * Tables as work crews become gangs of forced labor, with the head as overseer.
  * Weighted voting survives, but weighted by property instead of headcount.
  * National heroes become deified kings.
  * A lending bank and tokens become a tool for trapping farmers in debt.

Larger arcs:

* **The coming drought.**  The founders know that around 6100 HE the climate dries sharply: the Sahara turns to desert and Mesopotamia faces drought.  The federation has a thousand years' warning.  Does it tell its neighbors, prepare for refugees, or keep quiet to avoid seeming like prophets?
* **The last unabsorbed band.**  Every new township takes land some hunter-gatherer group used.  A ranger has to deal with a band that refuses to join, which raises the federation's own uncomfortable question: is its peaceful expansion just a gentler form of dispossession?
* **A plea for alliance.**  A mainland ruler asks the federation for help against an aggressive neighbor, maybe one that has copied federation drill.  The no-expansion rule says no; the ethics books may say otherwise.
* **The first purified penicillin.**  Centuries after the arrival, a chemist finally follows the medicine book's instructions, and the founders' promise "when the time comes" is fulfilled, with all the questions about who gets it first.
* **Moving zero longitude.**  The founders put zero longitude on the highest peak, but once signals and clocks are good enough, the capital, the ports, and the university all want the national observatory, and with it the nation's time: a petty fight with real prestige at stake.
* **Bead money.**  The federation is the only maker of glass, and its glass beads become a currency abroad, the way cowrie shells and Venetian trade beads did in our history.  Merchants who think long term sell only enough beads each year to keep pace with a growing economy and fend off local deflation, keeping the beads' value stable and earning a steady profit, arguably giving their neighbors sound money.  But any single merchant profits by selling more this year, and if they all do, the beads inflate and the trade collapses.  Holding the line takes coordination: beads become a restricted export, with a yearly quota set by the Board of Macroeconomics from the bead prices that merchants report from abroad, and who gets the quota becomes a political fight and a temptation to corruption.  The monopoly also has an end: once a neighbor learns to make glass (in our history, around 7500 HE), counterfeit beads flood in, and the federation loses control of a currency that other peoples depend on.

Personal stories:

* **The guest scholar who goes home.**  A Mesopotamian guest scholar returns from Cyprus with writing, numerals, and the ethics books, and tries to change their own city, possibly becoming the founder of a twisted copy without meaning to.
* **A census fraud.**  Because voting weight follows headcount, a town or table has a motive to pad its numbers.  A magistrate untangles it using birth tablets and thumbprint medallions, essentially early fingerprint forensics.
* **The head who won't step aside.**  A table's consensus breaks down, but its head still controls its work assignments and assembly vote.  The informal system that usually works has no clear answer.
* **A refugee crew.**  A refugee group builds the next planned town, then has to win the right to live in it alongside the volunteer tables from the parent town.

Small scenes:

* **The last founder's obituary.**  The family composes the list of accomplishments, reads it at the table, and watches it fired.  Living memory of the future ends.
* **A night watch at a relay station.**  A ranger on a peak, polishing mirrors, waiting for dawn and the first flash from across the island.
* **A child counting to 255** on their fingers for the first time.
* **The night before inspection.**  In the neolithic era, a kid who chipped his stone axe head is regrinding the edge the night before weekly militia practice.  In the iron age, there is a line at the sharpening stone.

Tensions noted along the way that could also drive stories:

* The near two-class split in the cities, between the "town inside" and everyone else.
* An embassy and its merchant quarter slowly becoming the overseas commitment the nation swore off.
* The Directorate needing money in a hurry, and the Board of Macroeconomics pushing back.
* The navy and marines as the most likely source of a would-be Caesar: full-time, mobile, often far from home, and with their own culture and loyalties.
* Federal examinations drifting into rote memorization of the founding books.
* Admiration of national heroes drifting into worship.
* A neighbor discovering iron.
* **Fuel versus the forests.**  Nearly every technology the founders bring burns wood: copper and later iron smelting, glass furnaces, lime kilns for plaster and mortar, brick and pottery kilns, and even the kilns that fire the clay books and records the federation exists to keep.  In our history, copper smelting helped strip ancient Cyprus of its forests.  The rush to build an iron industry in every town is the sharpest case, but the pressure is constant, and it is the real test of the federation's "a tree for a tree" ethic and its forest stewardship laws and procedures.  If the federation can't get it right, the island ends up with no fuel at all.
* Anyone with a kiln can fire a book, but someone still decides which books are copied widely and accepted into the town libraries and national vaults, and so which will survive.

## Appendix: Mathematics

This appendix describes the founders' mathematics book in more detail (see The Books).

Hexadecimal makes calculation, accounting, and record-keeping far easier than tally systems, and it makes halving easy.  People count to 255 on their fingers in binary, with the four fingers of each hand (not the thumb) giving one hex digit.  Geometry is taught for surveying fields, laying out towns, and building irrigation.  The book includes a simple standard notation for operations, variables, and functions, using a few symbols: + − × / ( ) , = < > ¬ { } ∈ ⊆ ⊂.  Negation does the work of extra symbols: "not equal" is written ¬=, "greater than or equal" is ¬<, and "less than or equal" is ¬>.  Everything else is written as words or abbreviated words, such as sqrt, pow, log, exp, and, or, union, and intersect.  Variable names can be several letters long, as in computer programming, so multiplication is always written out: uv is a single variable, and u × v is a product.  The notation is strictly linear, a single line of symbols with no superscripts, subscripts, or stacked fractions: a power is written pow(x, 2), not x²; a fraction is written a / b on one line, and the terms of a sequence are written as a function of an integer, x(1), x(2), and so on, rather than with subscripts.  That way every formula can be written in clay or ink, or sent by signal, exactly like ordinary text.

## Appendix: Music Notation

This appendix describes the notation in the founders' music book (see The Books).

Pitches are counted in semitones (twelve equal steps to the octave).  The head of each piece gives a reference pitch as an absolute number: its count of semitones above a zero point four octaves below the standard pitch, so that the standard pitch is 0x30.  The standard pitch is 0x240 vibrations per second, kept in the vault as a brass tuning fork.  Every note in the piece is then written as its distance in semitones from the reference, with negative numbers for notes below it.  The reference is usually the piece's home note, wherever the music has one, so that a scale or a tune looks the same in every key, and transposing a piece means changing only the reference.  Each event is written as a list of pitches in parentheses, separated by commas, followed by a comma and its duration in beats, with sung syllables placed before it; for example, a two-beat major chord on the reference pitch is written (0,4,7),2 (in hex, as all the numbers in the notation are).  A single note is a one-note chord, and a rest is an empty one.  Events are separated by interpuncts (see Records), and measures by double interpuncts, so that separate voices line up and every measure can be checked against the meter.  The meter is written as the grouping of beats in a measure, such as (3,3), and the tempo as beats per second, with the beat chosen as the smallest unit that divides every note evenly.  Pitches between the semitones are written as fractions, using the radix point, and music for a different scale can use its own numbering, as long as the document says how its numbers map onto pitch.  The compact form is used for books, archives, and signaling; musicians are taught to copy it onto wax tablets or slates in whatever layout suits their instrument.

## Appendix: Units

This appendix gives the rest of the founders' system of weights and measures (see The Books).

### Definitions

Formally, the system rests on four exact constants of nature, as modern SI does:

* **The second** is exactly 0x2D25C32D0 (12,119,192,272) periods of the cesium-133 hyperfine transition.
* **The reach** is defined by fixing the speed of light at exactly c = 0xDA00000 (228,589,568) reaches per second.
* **The unit of mass** is defined by fixing the reduced Planck constant at exactly ℏ = 0x31 × 16⁻³⁰.
* **The degree of temperature** is defined by fixing the Boltzmann constant at exactly k = 0x835 × 16⁻²².

None of these can be measured for millennia, so the founders' brass standards, made precisely to these definitions before they leave, are approximations to use until exact measurements become possible.  The system is deliberately flexible: good-enough approximations can be made with neolithic tools, while the exact definitions will still be valid in a thousand years, so that the units never need to be redefined.

### Time

For short intervals, a pendulum one reach long gives a good approximate second, accurate to a fraction of a percent if it is made carefully: a small, heavy bob on a light cord, the length measured from the pivot to the center of the bob, and small swings.  The federation's units of time reuse the familiar names, but with different lengths:

| Unit       | Fraction of a day | Length in our units | Use                               |
|------------|-------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **hour**   | 1/16              | 90 minutes          | work shifts, guard watches        |
| **degree** | 1/256             | ~5.6 minutes        | scheduling                        |
| **second** | 1/65536           | ~1.3 seconds        | pulse rates, pendulums, experiments |

A square 64 reaches on a side, about 1.22 hectares (three acres), is the standard field unit for surveying and allotting land.

### Volume and mass

Sixteen jugs make about 20 liters, the size of a small amphora; a cup, a quarter jug, is about 314 mL, the size of a drinking cup; and a spoon, 1/256 jug, is about 4.9 mL, almost exactly a modern teaspoon.

A jug of water has a mass of about 1.26 kg, the base unit of mass.  The value of ℏ was chosen to make the unit of mass 1.26159 kg, which matches a jug of water to within 0.03%, less than the change in water's density between a cold and a warm day; in practice, it is the founders' brass mass standard.  In factors of 4:

| 1/16          | 79 g      | kitchen and market scales               |

### Angle

The base unit of angle is the **turn**, one full circle, just as the base unit of everyday time is the day; hours, degrees, and seconds are fractions of a turn.  (In calculus, this puts a factor of τ = 2π in the derivative: if x is measured in turns, the derivative of sin x is τ cos x.)  A degree is 1/256 of a turn (about 1.4 of our degrees).  An angle of 1/16 of a circle (0x10 degrees, or 22.5 of our degrees) is always called an hour, since the sky turns through that angle in one hour, just as astronomers in our world call 15° an hour.  Likewise, the sky turns one degree of angle in one degree of time, which is why the federation uses the same name for both: hour, degree, and second each mean the same fraction of a circle or of a day.  The first hex digit of an angle, 1/16 of a circle, matches the 16 points of a traditional compass rose (N, NNE, NE, ENE, E, and so on).

### Longitude and standard time

Zero longitude runs through the highest peak on Cyprus (our Mount Olympus, in the Troodos mountains), chosen because it is unambiguous, and the planned site of a national observatory that will keep the nation's time once signals can be sent over long distances (see Signaling).  Later generations are free to choose a new zero point.  The astronomy book recommends switching from apparent solar time (sundial time) to mean solar time once accurate clocks make it feasible.

### Temperature

Temperature exists only in the book at first: nobody can measure it until thermometers can be made, which requires glass.  The scale the book defines is absolute: absolute zero is 0, and water freezes at 0x100 (256), to within a hundredth of a degree.  (Formally, the degree is defined by the Boltzmann constant; the value of k was chosen to put water's freezing point at 0x100.)  One degree is about 1.067 °C, close to a Celsius degree.

| Temperature              | Celsius   | Value |
|--------------------------|-----------|-------|
| Absolute zero            | −273 °C   | 0x000 |
| Water freezes            | 0 °C      | 0x100 |
| Pleasant day             | 20 °C     | 0x113 |
| Normal body temperature  | 37 °C     | 0x123 |
| Hot summer day           | 40 °C     | 0x125 |
| Water boils (sea level)  | 100 °C    | 0x15E |

The book also describes the experiment for finding absolute zero (measuring how a trapped gas's pressure changes between freezing and boiling, and extending the line back to zero pressure), so that the first scholar with good glassware can check it.

### Electricity (future)

Like temperature, electrical units exist only in the book until they are needed.  The book defines them without any new base unit, by fixing the magnetic constant at μ₀ = 16⁻⁵ (0x0.00001).  This power of 16 is chosen so that the electrical units come out at practical sizes, much as SI's old value of 4π × 10⁻⁷ was chosen to make the ampere and volt convenient:

| Unit       | Size in SI  |
|------------|-------------|
| Current    | ≈ 0.98 A    |
| Voltage    | ≈ 1.7 V     |
| Resistance | ≈ 1.7 Ω     |
| Power      | ≈ 1.6 W     |

The unit of force behind these is one jug accelerated at one reach per second per second, about 1.26 newtons.  (A useful fact the book points out: because the reach is so close to a seconds pendulum, the acceleration of gravity is about 9.86 reaches per second per second, close to π².)

**The experiment that defines the unit of current.**  Two wires carrying current in the same direction attract each other.  For two long, straight, parallel conductors, the force is

  force = μ₀ × current² × length / (2π × distance apart)

To make the force large enough to measure on a balance:

1. Wind two identical rectangular coils of 64 turns each, so that one long side of each coil is a straight bundle of 64 wires, 1 reach long.
2. Fix one coil flat on a table.  Hang the other from one arm of a sensitive balance, directly above the first, so that their long sides are parallel and 1/64 reach apart.  Keep the other sides of the coils well away from each other.
3. Connect the two coils one after the other, so the same current flows through both, in the same direction along the facing sides.  The hanging coil is pulled down toward the fixed one.
4. Balance the hanging coil with no current flowing, then turn the current on and add masses to the other arm until the balance is restored.

At exactly one unit of current, the force between the coils is 16⁻⁵ × 64² × 64 / 2π = 1/(8π) ≈ 0.0398 units of force, or about 0.050 newtons.  On the balance, that is matched by a mass of about 1.03/256 of a jug, or about 5.1 grams.  (Converting force to mass uses the local acceleration of gravity, about 9.86 reaches per second per second, since here a magnetic force is being balanced against the pull of gravity on a mass.)  Since the force goes as the square of the current, any current, in units, is the square root of (the mass needed to balance it / that 5-gram mass).  (The book gives small corrections for the ends of the bundles and the far sides of the coils.)

### Constants

The book gives a table of physical constants in the federation's units, so that future scholars can use them without converting.  Those marked exact are fixed by definition; the rest are measured values, given to three hex digits.  (Masses are in jugs, the unit of mass.)

| Constant | Value                 | Units                      |
|----------|-----------------------|----------------------------|
| c        | 0xDA00000 (exact)     | reaches per second         |
| ℏ        | 0x31 × 16⁻³⁰ (exact)  | jug · reach² per second    |
| k        | 0x835 × 16⁻²² (exact) | energy per degree          |
| μ₀       | 16⁻⁵ (exact)          | force per current²         |
| G        | 0x1.F2 × 16⁻⁹         | reach³ per jug per second² |
| e        | 0x2.4C × 16⁻¹⁶        | current × second           |

Since ℏ, μ₀, and c are exact, the elementary charge is fixed by the fine-structure constant α, a pure number (about 1/137) that no choice of units can change: e = sqrt(4π × α × ℏ / (μ₀ × c)).

### Keeping the standards

The founders' brass standards are kept in the national vaults (in the first town's vault until the capital is built), and copies in fired clay, brass, or stone are made for every town.  Clay shrinks in the kiln, so clay copies must be measured against a brass standard after firing, and ground to size; stone copies are ground to size the same way.

## Appendix: The Table House

This appendix describes the table house in more detail (see Tables).

Each table lives in a courtyard house, with rooms arranged around a central open courtyard.  The lot is about 66 × 82 ft (about 5,400 sq ft), with about 3,200 sq ft of rooms on the ground floor and a courtyard of roughly 2,000 sq ft, where much of daily life takes place.  Except when it rains, the courtyard feels like one more room of the house, the largest one.  Because the shared rooms serve all twenty people and are in use all day, this is about 200 sq ft of indoor space per person, generous by ancient standards.

* **The hall** (about 650 sq ft): one long table seating the whole table, used for meals, table meetings, ceremonies, and winter evenings.  The family vault, a niche or shelves holding the family's obituary tablets (see Documentation), is here.
* **The kitchen**, with a bread oven, opening onto the courtyard; in summer, cooking moves outdoors.
* **Family rooms** (about 12 × 13 ft each) on the ground floor, one for each couple or family with small children.
* **Small bedrooms upstairs** (about 8 × 10 ft each) for older children, single adults, and elders, opening onto a gallery overlooking the courtyard.
* **A storeroom** for oil and wine jars, the table's flour and other staples from the granary, and dried fruit.
* **A workroom** for looms and spinning.
* **A washroom** for bathing, next to the kitchen, so that water heated on the hearth or bread oven only has to be carried a few steps.  Bathing is mostly washing: sitting on a stool and pouring water from a jug over a drain in the floor.
* **A latrine room**, separate from the washroom, with seats for two or three people at a time, a urinal draining into a sealed jar, and a handwashing basin with soap or wood ash just outside the door.  It is built at the corner of the house nearest the drain to the shared cesspit, just downhill from the washroom.  Since water is precious, used bathwater and washing water are reused: to flush the latrine drain, and to water the courtyard vine and plants.  Any excess runs into the street drains, which carry rainwater and wastewater outside the walls.
* **A place for militia gear**, where each member's weapons are kept ready for weekly inspection.
* **A small pen** at the back for a few goats, and later a donkey.
* **A cistern** under the courtyard, filled by rain from the roofs.
* **A flat roof**, used for drying fruit and flax and for sleeping on hot summer nights, and a vine on a pergola shading part of the courtyard.

The house is built on stone foundations, with mud-brick walls at first and fired brick later, reinforced with bands of timber against earthquakes, plastered and whitewashed with lime.  Every room with a hearth has a chimney or smoke flue, one of the founders' quietest and most valuable health improvements.

## Appendix: Inks

These are the ink recipes from the book on writing and records (see Records).

* **Carbon ink**, for everyday writing with a pen: soot from lamps or burned resin, ground fine and mixed with water and a plant gum (from almond or cherry trees) as a binder.  It is cheap, easy to make, and never fades, but it sits on the surface of the paper and can be washed or scraped off.
* **Iron gall ink**, for contracts, records, and anything that must not be altered: oak galls (from Cyprus's oaks) soaked in water or wine, mixed with green vitriol (iron sulfate, found as a mineral near Cyprus's copper mines) and gum.  It soaks into the paper and darkens as it dries, so it can't be erased without leaving a mark.  Over centuries it slowly eats into the paper, which doesn't matter for paper records meant to last a decade.
* **Printing ink**: lampblack ground into linseed oil (from flax) that has been boiled until thick, like a varnish.  Water-based inks bead up and run off metal type, but an oil ink sticks to the type and transfers cleanly to paper.
* **Red ink**, from red ochre in gum water, for headings, corrections, and the margin notes of proofreaders, so that they stand out from the text they comment on.
* **Seal ink**, a thick paste of ochre or soot in oil, for stamping town seals onto paper documents.
* **Slip paint** for clay: a thin, iron-rich clay slip, painted onto a tablet before firing, which fires to a permanent dark color, for labeling tablets and highlighting impressed text.

## Appendix: Obituaries

This appendix describes the obituary tablets in more detail (see Documentation).

The obituary tablet gives the person's name (including pronunciation diacritics), the place and day of their birth and death, and their parents and children (with the children's birth dates and places), as well as the list of their accomplishments in life.  The town administrators check this information against the person's birth certificate, a copy of which is kept in the town vault; for immigrants, a naturalization document, recorded when they are adopted into a table, takes its place.  The administrators then produce three copies of the tablet, each with the town seal, and fire them in the kiln.  One copy is returned to the family, and over the generations, families build up family vaults to store them.  Anyone can commission an additional copy, so when relatives end up in different tables or towns, each branch of the family can keep its own.  The second copy is kept in the town vault, and the third is sent to the capital to be placed in the national vaults.
